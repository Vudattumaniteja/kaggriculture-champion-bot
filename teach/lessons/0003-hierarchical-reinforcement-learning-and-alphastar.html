Lesson 0003 • Plain English • Hierarchical RL & AlphaStar

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL)

How DeepMind's AlphaStar, OpenAI Five, and our farming bot solve massive strategy games using two-tier Manager-Worker architecture.

Core Learning Objective: Learn how grandmaster game AIs tackle impossibly large decision spaces (over $10^{13}$ choices every second) by separating high-level strategic thinking (the Macro Manager) from lightning-fast tactical execution (the Micro Worker).

Part 1: The Non-Technical Guide (Intuition First)

1. The "CEO vs. The Factory Floor" Analogy

Imagine a large company with 500 factory workers.

If the CEO tries to tell every single worker which foot to step with, which wrench to pick up, and which screw to turn every second, two things happen:

  1. The CEO has a nervous breakdown from information overload.
  2. The company goes bankrupt because no one is planning the long-term business strategy.

How real companies work (Hierarchical Division of Labor):

  • The CEO (Macro Manager): Looks at the market once a month. Decides: "We need 40% electric cars, 60% trucks, and we should expand into Europe."
  • The Factory Foreman (Micro Solver): Takes the CEO's targets and assigns specific workers to specific assembly stations using an optimal schedule.

In Kaggriculture: If our neural network tries to control every single step of 5 farmhands across a 10×10 grid for 720 turns, there are over $30,000,000,000,000$ possible move combinations every turn. The AI gets overwhelmed.

Instead, our AI acts like the CEO (deciding crop heatmaps and animal quotas), while an instant math algorithm (the Hungarian Solver) acts like the foreman, assigning chores with zero traffic jams.

2. How AlphaStar & OpenAI Five Solved It

In StarCraft II, DeepMind faced the exact same problem with AlphaStar: thousands of soldiers, buildings, and mineral miners.

AlphaStar split its brain into two layers:

  • Strategic Layer (Macro): Decides the high-level battle plan (e.g. "Build 5 Stalkers, research Blink tech, and expand to the bottom-left base").
  • Tactical Layer (Micro): Pointer networks that execute instant dodging, firing, and pathfinding on individual units.

How the CEO Communicates with the Staff (The Heatmap Language)

How does the AI CEO tell the workers what to do without micromanaging?

It outputs Spatial Heatmaps (like a weather map showing where rain is likely):

  • Melon Heatmap: Bright green over fertile top-left tiles, dark over rocks.
  • Water Heatmap: Bright red on tiles that are drying out today.

The micro workers simply look at the heatmap and march toward the brightest tiles. Clean, simple, and collision-free.

Part 2: The Technical Architecture & Math

Now let us look at the mathematical formulation of Two-Tier Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

1. Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP)

In HRL, the environment is modeled as a two-time-scale Semi-Markov Decision Process: 

Macro-Step T (State S_T)
       │
       ▼ [Manager Policy: g_T ~ pi_M(g | S_T)]
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Subgoal / Macro-Potential g_T                                │
│                                                             │
│   t=0: Worker a_0 ~ pi_W(a | s_0, g_T) ──► Env step ──► r_0  │
│   t=1: Worker a_1 ~ pi_W(a | s_1, g_T) ──► Env step ──► r_1  │
│   ...                                                       │
│   t=c-1: Worker a_{c-1} ~ pi_W(· | ·, g_T) ──► Env ──► r_{c-1}│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
       │
       ▼ Aggregate Return R_T^M = sum_{k=0}^{c-1} gamma^k r_{t+k}

2. Why Policy Gradients Work Over Non-Differentiable Micro Solvers

In our system, the low-level micro solver is the Hungarian Bipartite Matching Algorithm ($\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ via scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment).

You might wonder: "The Hungarian algorithm uses discrete 'if/else' matching logic. How can PyTorch compute gradients through it?"

The Policy Gradient Theorem:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{\text{macro}} \mid s) \cdot \hat{A}(s, \mathbf{a}_{\text{macro}}) \right]$$ The gradient flows strictly through the probabilities of the macro decisions ($\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$). We do not need to backpropagate through the physical simulator or the Hungarian matcher. The matcher is simply part of the external environment engine.

3. Comparison: Classical HRL vs. Our Hybrid Architecture

Feature Classical HRL (FeUdal / HIRO / PPOC) Our Neural Macro + Hungarian Solver
Worker Sample Efficiency Poor (Needs 10M+ steps to learn walking and basic chores) Instant $\mathcal{O}(1)$ (Zero training steps needed for micro execution)
Worker Non-Stationarity Severe (Worker policy shifts constantly while Manager learns) Zero (Hungarian solver is stationary and mathematically optimal)
Collision Avoidance Stochastic (Workers frequently bump into each other) Guaranteed (Deterministic Manhattan reservation grid)
Strategic Adaptability High (Neural Manager explores continuous goal space) High (Neural Policy emits continuous crop & market heatmaps)

4. Stabilizing Multi-Head PPO with GradNorm

Our neural network outputs multiple decisions simultaneously (Crop Heatmaps, Workforce, Land Expansion, Market Fractions, Value Critic).

To prevent one loss from overpowering the others, we use GradNorm (Chen et al., 2018): 

# Dynamically balances task loss weights w_i based on gradient norms:
G_i(t) = || grad_W ( w_i(t) * L_i(t) ) ||_2
Target_i = Mean_G * ( L_i(t) / Initial_L_i )^alpha
Update w_i to minimize |G_i(t) - Target_i|

Summary & What We Learned

  1. Macro vs Micro: Splitting strategy (Macro Manager) from execution (Micro Solver) collapses a $10^{13}$ action space down to simple, manageable decisions.
  2. The Heatmap Bridge: The neural policy communicates with workers by painting 2D spatial priority heatmaps.
  3. Hybrid Dominance: Combining a neural strategic brain with a deterministic mathematical solver eliminates sample waste and guarantees zero worker collisions.
